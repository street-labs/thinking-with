---
title: 'The Pleasure of Being Persuaded'
description: On learning rhetoric to avoid being duped — and then learning, against the grain of critique, to enjoy it.
date: 2026-01-30
author: Nathaniel Street
tags: ['essays', 'rhetoric']
---

When I teach introduction to rhetoric courses, I begin with something of a pitch. I tell my students that they should learn rhetoric so that they're less likely to get duped in life. We are all inundated by wave after wave of advertisements, social media posts, political discourse, and family drama. I describe "persuasion" as a constellation of forces that push and pull on us to a greater or lesser degree rather than as the deliberate attempt to consciously change someone's minds or actions about something. These forces are working on us all the time, usually in ways that we can't easily see or understand.

What's more, I tell my students that *they themselves* are constantly working to persuade everyone around them. That is, they are part of a constellation of forces that push and pull on those around them to a greater or lesser degree, usually in ways that they can't easily see or understand.

So learn rhetoric, I tell them. Learn to see what Aristotle calls "the available means of persuasion in any given case." Learn to analyze the forces in your own world so you're more aware of what's moving you at any given moment. Learn to write, and speak and think, more consciously of how you push and pull on people around you. I tell my students that this is more than just learning to make friends and influence people. There is an ethical dimension to all this rhetorical awareness, because at the end of the day it's about how you relate to others.

Here's what I don't tell them. In all the years that I've been teaching and studying rhetoric, I think more and more that "being aware" isn't simply a good thing. I mean, I'm glad that I can do it. I do like being able to step back and critically examine someone's line of thinking, or attend to how emotions are affecting how the world shows up for me, or consider how my social relationship with someone adjusts how I'm able to interpret their actions and words. But behind all that analysis and awareness often lies a different disposition, a different mood, that Rita Felski describes in her book, *The Limits of Critique*, as a certain kind of suspicion. The rhetorical awareness can easily feel like it's a tool for keeping the world from getting the better of you. Felski, pretty convincingly, argues that a lot of the affective and rhetorical components of most critique is sort of borrowed and adapted from criminology. Critique often positions us to track down some crime or criminal. Learn rhetoric so you're less likely to get duped in life – so that you're less likely to get taken advantage of or violated. Learn rhetoric so you're less likely to be the victim of a crime.

Felski's entire book basically argues that there's nothing necessarily wrong with this; there's nothing wrong with being suspicious of the world around us. Her beef is that it seems like this is the only way we can intellectually engage with the world. Critique. A "hermeneutics of suspicion." Has dominated our intellectual lives – especially in the university. And she'd like to imagine that we can do something else.

I do too. I'll only hint at this with students, especially in intro classes, but I **like** getting persuaded. I like getting seduced. I even like, occasionally, getting duped. I don't think that these are simply bad things to be avoided through a heightened rhetorical awareness. They are fun. They are enjoyable. And they can even be pleasurable. I joke that nearly 20 years of teaching rhetoric has made me, occasionally, **more** susceptible to advertising. Sometimes I want Taco Bell exclusively because of the ad! And this isn't just some intellectual appreciation for a marketing team putting together an admirable campaign. I want it. And I delight in wanting it.

We typically think of critique, along with analysis and theory, in terms of a distance from the world. We zoom out to get a "better" handle on things. From the critical heights, we're less likely to be contaminated by all the complicated things that bewilder us down on the ground. We rise above the dust cloud. But isn't this just another way of saying that critique is a way to keep us from being affected by the world? And isn't "being affected by the world" just another way to say "living in the world?" At a certain point, critical distance starts to sound like a kind of living death. You're not technically dead, but you've deadened your ability to be affected by the world. There are good reasons to call the critics kill-joys.

I don't want to stop here. I don't want to end with a dualism between critically distanced un-death on the one hand and foolish life on the other hand. Like I said, I think that practicing rhetoric has, over the years, **taught me** to enjoy being persuaded. It's taught me, not just to be more aware of the persuasive forces that surround me, not just to develop an appreciation for the artifice of a well constructed argument, but it's taught me to indulge in being pushed and pulled on by those forces. To get here, I had to pass through a more analytic, critical, and even suspicious orientation to persuasion. And I didn't just leave that orientation behind to get to some new, more evolved, pleasurable relationship to persuasion. I still have, more or less, that critical edge – and I don't think I could lose it if I tried. But that's not all there is. And critical suspicion isn't even all that critical suspicion can be or do.

So yeah, learn rhetoric so you're less likely to get duped in life. Then keep learning it so that you learn to enjoy being duped all the more.
